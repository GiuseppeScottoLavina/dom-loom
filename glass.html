Issue 12 · Oceans

Tides of light

How the sea takes the sun apart, and puts it back together at the bottom of a rock pool.

At low tide the pools along the shelf hold a few centimetres of water and the whole of the sky. Lean over one at noon and the floor of it is drawn in moving gold: thin bright lines that knot and part, never still for longer than a breath.

Those lines are caustics. Every ripple on the surface is a lens, and every lens gathers a little of the sunlight that falls on it and throws it somewhere a little to the side. Where two ripples aim at the same patch of sand, the light piles up. Where they aim away, the sand goes dim.

A wave does the same thing at a larger scale. The crest is thicker than the trough, so light that enters it is bent harder, and a swell rolling over a sandbar paints a slow lattice that you can watch from a cliff.

“The surface is a lens that never stops being ground.”

Further down, the colours go one by one. Red is gone within ten metres; orange and yellow follow. By forty metres a diver's blood looks green, and the sea is one blue that has given everything else away.

None of this needs a reef or a camera. It needs a sunny day, a pool the size of a sink, and the patience to keep your shadow off it. The light does the rest, the way it always has.

— Mara Elling, from the shelf at Porth Tide
